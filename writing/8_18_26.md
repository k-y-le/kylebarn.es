there’s this text on the back of the chobani vanilla blended nonfat greek yogurt pint container (wow … so many words, that), which is kinda long so i copied it below. i keep looking at it when i make my daily breakfast, and i know that it’s corporate copy so it isn’t meant to mean anything at all, but i just can’t get past the ease with which this kind of writing waves away the complicated nature by which a vanilla bean arrives in our lives. product design and advertising are particularly adept at this kind of obfuscation of global supply chains.

“Carried from some far-off exotic place, where a little flower became a little bean. And that little bean, suspended and unremarkable, is the cloak that conceals the magic within. Flavor like perfume, folded up in earthen envelopes, rich and warm and wonderful. Gently opening up like the blossom that began it all.”

in delivering neatly arranged rows of products, the supermarket performs a kind of magic. enormous efforts are put towards invisibilizing the layers of labor that make a pint of vanilla yogurt end up in my fridge, from the unloading of groceries in the early mornings to truck drivers and container ships and packaging and farming ingredients and milking cows and more and more that i don’t even really know about. to follow this single pint of yogurt might take me pages of writing. i’m probably not even be allowed to know the whole story.

this is, of course, cause for concern, but it’s also the life we’re dealing with. grocery store guilt is a familiar feeling for me, and yet it has not been strong enough to dramatically change our behavior (my behavior). each day we find ourselves entangled in all sorts of ethical conundrums, living out the contradictions that capitalism has woven all around us. these threads derive from exploitation and alienation, and so its up to us what we want to do about that. lately, i’ve been thinking of my own capacity to pick at and unravel some of these threads almost like a video game. i have a certain number of points assigned to my character, which i can spend towards living a healthy, ethical, mutualistic life.

but i certainly can’t do it all. one of the ways i’ve been clearing space in the knotted web of ethically asymmetrical relations that we call food is through consumption book club, which offers a bounded space for rethinking the consumer habits we unthinkingly reproduce. it’s also a fun collaborative creative project, one that engages my own curiosities when it comes to facilitation, design, writing, and — of course — food. i won’t make any grand claims about the ethical rerouting this project can accomplish; much of that is still to be seen, and it can be hard enough pulling off a series of this type of ambition. overall, though, it’s a vehicle by which i can investigate my own ethical entanglements with consumption, from the specific to the broad.

vanilla, in particular, has a compelling story of how it came to be folded into the global supply chain, tracing back to the 19th century (and surely before, but that’s where today’s story takes me). i’ve always enjoyed tracing the stories of these ecological beings that — owing most commonly to the hyper-specificity of centuries of evolution — resist the plantation form. the products we see on our grocery store shelves today are so much the result of centuries if not millenia-long cultivation processes, and following that history we can see all sorts of branching paths not taken. each of us could potentially have a drastically different diet, if monocrop agriculture hadn’t chosen a particular breed of corn as one of its crown jewels, or if one enslaved teenager in the 19th century hadn’t discovered how to hand-pollinate the vanilla orchid. so many bodies, today and throughout history, have been wrought to deliver me this pint of vanilla yogurt. i am, as are all of us, like a bull in a china shop. i can hardly move without the reverberations of my actions causing destruction around the world.

i have spent a while agonizing over this. i will likely continue to do so, as long as the society we live in serves up these feasts of contradiction. but hopefully i can nudge myself, little by little, towards something slightly better, slightly more manageable, and slightly more collective.

On Vanilla and Other Entanglements

If you find yourself face to face with *Vanilla planifolia*, be sure to greet it kindly. You are in the presence of one of this planet's most rare and sought-after beings, producer of the second most valuable spice in the world, bested only by saffron. In its own way, the vanilla orchid has resisted cultivation–it belongs to that category of orchid so uniquely adapted to a singular pollinator that it resists the totalizing logic of the plantation, the neat rows, the interchangeable seed. Only the Melipona bee, native to Mexico, has ever been observed pollinating this flower, the two species entwined in a fatalistic dance choreographed over millions of years. Forever companions, that is–until a twelve-year-old enslaved boy on the island of Réunion came along and rewrote the terms of the arrangement entirely.

Edmond Albius was born into slavery. Piecing together the colonialism-tinted archive of his life, I find myself speculating that plants were his escape, perhaps a retreat to his interior. His owner, a French botanist and colonist, taught him the rudiments of the discipline–how to read a plant the way you might read a face. And somewhere along that path of naming leaf couplings and tracing root structures and learning the quiet architecture of a stem, Edmond began to fertilize flowers by hand. Hand-pollination is exactly what it sounds like: using fingers where wind or wing would otherwise do the work, employed whenever natural pollination is undesirable, insufficient, or simply impossible. Today scientists reach for it as a lifeline for endangered plants, almost always because evolution has wound them into a place of impossibly specific entanglement–lose the pollinator, and the plant goes with it.

So it was with the vanilla orchid. Edmond either discovered or was ordered to discover a way to hand-pollinate it, and the discovering was no small feat–the flower survives only a few hours in the heat and the wet, and once it closes, it is gone for good, gone for the season, gone maybe forever. But Edmond prevailed, and in doing so revolutionized the flavor palette of the entire world. It was a windfall for Réunion, and Albius is still spoken of there as something close to legend. But let's be clear: this is no fairytale. He remained enslaved until France abolished slavery in its colonies in 1848, and he died in poverty in 1880, decades after handing the world one of its most treasured tastes.

We are all, always, making worlds around us. It happens that Edmond's worldmaking rippled outward–into the cafés of Paris, into the ice cream parlors of far-off cities he would never see–even while his own immediate experience remained locked in the grip of colonial conquest. Hand-pollination is still the method used today in cultivating the vanilla bean, the only edible fruit in the entire orchid family. Nature is full of these strange, exacting pairings. Millions of years of evolution deliver us hyper-specific adaptations, singular pollinators, relationships built for two and no one else. And when we try to remake that world in the image of industry–plantation, export tax, gross domestic product–we rub up hard against the tender intricacies of a system that was humming along long before our ancestors ever stood upright.

These pairings comprise a kind of queer companionship across the boundary of species, and we are caught up in it too, whether we notice or not. Queer as in different, as in strange and specific and slippery and subversive. Queer as in the willingness to attend, for days and months and years, to the particular mechanics of one orchid's pollination. In queer ecology, we look to the multispecies worlds all around us for a way out from beneath the crushing march of capital–and what do we find? Stories of human-orchid companionship. Ambivalent attachments bound up with survival and with sex. We find ourselves already inside the ongoing process of becoming-with the plants we eat and harvest, and the ones we don't. A queer ecological world opens up ethical obligations even toward the vine mimicking its neighbors on the floor of a Chilean forest, even toward the petal tuned with impossible precision to reflect a bat's echolocation click. If we are present enough, attentive enough, we might find that a new world is already happening all around us, has been happening the whole time. Let us look to the orchid, and find ourselves somewhere along the way.
