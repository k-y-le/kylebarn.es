in an effort to share more writing that isn’t just like, me being emo, here’s a long essay i wrote on the amtrak to boston, in preparation for alec’s infrastructure party, which imo was a huge success. i am often trying to come up with an excuse to talk about the salton sea, so the chance to present in front of a big room of strangers all about it was something i couldn’t give up. naturally i didn’t return to this essay between looking at it and presenting, so i just freestyled, but it’s nice to have a place for my various thoughts on the gorgeous insanity that is the history & future of the salton sea. anyways, if you want to learn a lil about a strange looming disaster and the contested water history of southern california, read on.

/***

on March 9, 2022, i gave a presentation not too dissimilar from this one. i can't find the slides anymore; i'd thought about reusing them, for nostalgia's sake. it was spring break of my final semester of undergrad, and four friends and i (two of whom just got married last weekend) had escaped the dregs of east coast winter for southern california. we'd booked a house near joshua tree national park, anticipating sunny vistas, hikes, delightful botany facts. but i was on a mission, unbeknownst to the others: i wanted to visit the salton sea.

convincing a group to drive over an hour to see a polluted natural disaster in the making is not necessarily straightforward. but i think i have a bit of a reputation for this kind of oddball desire, so they dutifully sat through my presentation, and it worked. the next day we decamped for the salton sea, in all its contradictory beauty.

the salton sea is not a sea, it's a lake. actually, at this point it's kind of several lakes, but we'll get there. it's the largest lake in california, about 35 miles long and 15 wide, over 300 square miles; you could fit manhattan inside it fourteen times. and like most places at the periphery of extraction and industry, it carries a complicated and contradictory history. that's precisely why I was so compelled to go, and why I've been back since. as a site of infrastructure both failed and too-successful, the salton sea is a vortex of the various projects of modernity, and one that foretells our future, for those willing to look squarely at it.

at one point the lake was far larger. as lake cahuilla, it stretched north past present-day indio and south across what is now the border into mexico: some 2,200 square miles, nearly seven times its current size. but the history of water is always fluid, and static borders are something of a contemporary phenomenon. for millennia the colorado river swung back and forth, sometimes emptying into this basin, sometimes into the gulf of california. the lake filled, dried to desert, and filled again. you can still see its old shorelines etched into the mountainsides. water is always seeking its way home, gravity bringing reunions with larger bodies.

![map of salton sea and lake cahuilla](https://d2w9rnfcy7mm78.cloudfront.net/50455550/original_725f99b59ad8d474a118b91c72ae5dd4.png?1789489973?bc=0)

and so we come to another contradiction of this place, still true today: the salton sea is a wetland within a desert, a salt lake over a dry basin. it has long been an essential part of southern california, though you're unlikely to hear about it should you visit los angeles. the Cahuilla peoples lived, fished, and gathered food along its shores, as did others. And birds have long used it as a breeding ground and stopover, a welcome respite on the long flight over an inhospitable landscape.

the salton sea as we know it is incredibly recent (geologically, a blink), formed between 1905 and 1907. the sea is, essentially, a mistake: the bastard child of settler hubris and of the water wars that continue to shape the region today. in 1900, work began on a canal to divert the colorado river toward farms in the imperial valley. here grows dates, lettuce, alfalfa; the region is famous for its date shakes. the canal was poorly built and choked with silt, so engineers cut a new opening in the riverbank to boost the flow. in 1905, spring floods tore right through it. for nearly two years, almost the entire colorado river poured into the dry bed of lake cahuilla while engineers scrambled to keep the whole valley from going under. thus the salton sea was born: water going where it wants.

what I'm interested in here is what worlds are borne from infrastructure, and how failures beget opportunities, shaping landscapes both literal and cultural. anywhere you see water infrastructure, you'll find leaks. it's inevitable; ask any water engineer. and anywhere there are leaks, there are opportunists: weeds sprouting, whole micro-ecosystems forming around that precious, precious resource, a consistent supply of water. that is, until it dries up.

the salton sea is a saline endorheic lake, which means two things. it's salty, today nearly twice as salty as the ocean. and it has no outflow. whatever enters stays, and waits to evaporate. in that sense, it acts as a kind of ledger of everything people in and around the valley would rather forget.

after the flood, the lake should have dried up, if not for nearby farmers, who used generous amounts of water (remember, this is a desert) to irrigate their fields. colorado river water carries salt, so farmers deliberately over-water to flush it past the roots. and what do you do with all that excess once your thirsty date palms have had their fill? divert it into the conveniently located water dump, of course. and so the salton sea held its size, evaporation offset by dozens of drains carrying runoff and everything dissolved in it.

the mid-20th century was the golden age of the salton sea. marketed as the Salton Riviera, it drew developers who built resort towns like salton city and bombay beach, and celebrities who came to fish, race speedboats, and sunbathe in the desert heat. here's another contradiction: resort destination and irrigation mistake, all at once. humans, too, are opportunists. among them were resort owners, fishermen (the state stocked the sea with fish to encourage recreation), farmers, health resorts (the dry air was thought to cure consumption), and the military.

the navy opened a seaplane base on the western shore in 1942. In 1944 and '45, the crew that would fly the enola gay trained here, dropping dummy atomic bombs onto a white raft floating on the water. one fat man prototype was reportedly dropped by accident near the town of calipatria; it failed to explode. over the following decades came more than a thousand missile and bomb tests: the salton riviera and the atomic age, sharing a shoreline. the base was eventually abandoned, and the rising sea swallowed its runway. water, again, going where it wants. those not included in the flourishing were the poor and Indigenous locals, who, as is so often the case in the colonization of the west, were dispossessed of their land through bogus claims, murders, and unfair deals.

but, surprise surprise, the golden age was destined to end. by the '70s, scientists were warning that the water was getting worse. all that runoff was dense with fertilizer and pesticides, and with selenium leached from the soil. evaporation only concentrates what's left behind. fertilizer feeds algae; the algae bloom and die, and their decay strips the water of oxygen. another contradiction: a body of water in which fish suffocate. when water gets sick, everyone suffers, human and nonhuman, and indicator species, those considered most disposable, are the first to go.

by the '80s and '90s the sea saw mass die-offs, with tens of thousands, sometimes millions, of fish washed up dead on the shore. the state paid laborers to round up the corpses and burned the piles when they grew too high. this is when the salton sea earned its still-current reputation as, well, stinky. naturally, mounds of rotting fish weren't enticing to wealthy tourists seeking rest and relaxation. this was the death knell of regional tourism, and the economy duly began to collapse.

the birds followed. in 1992, some 150,000 eared grebes died of a cause no one could identify. in 1996, avian botulism killed more than 15,000 fish-eating birds, among them nearly 10,000 white pelicans and over 1,200 endangered brown pelicans: the deadliest pelican die-off ever recorded. biologists ran an incinerator around the clock and still couldn't keep up. in the bodies of the dead they found selenium and DDE, the residue of DDT, which had been banned more than two decades earlier. and so the salton sea became a wildlife refuge marked primarily by dead wildlife, not living: a refuge that poisons.

so naturally, the salton sea is a perfect place to visit. actually, when i visited, the air was fairly clear and the smell hardly troubling. there haven't been any fish left to die for a while now. the sea continues to shrink, year by year. since 2003, when a water deal began sending the valley's water to coastal cities instead, the surface has dropped about ten feet, exposing dozens of square miles of lakebed.

and what's left? we're back to a dry desert basin, but this time it's laced with the leftovers: selenium, cadmium, pesticide residue. dust, no longer bound to the water it used to call companion, likes to billow and bellow its way into giant storms, some drifting west toward los angeles. nearby, asthma-related ER visits run at twice the state average. it's a reminder of the extractive past and dystopian present that haunt the great project of building a city of millions on land that could only ever reasonably support a fraction of that.

this is another reason to study and visit the salton sea. here’s anna tsing, elaine gan, heather swanson, and nils bubandt: "ghosts point to our forgetting, showing us how living landscapes are imbued with earlier tracks and traces… ghosts remind us that we live in an impossible present — a time of rupture, a world haunted with the threat of extinction." and the salton sea, where infrastructure's success has facilitated both flourishing and destruction, seems to me a prime example of a ghostly landscape.

but the salton sea's story doesn't end there. like all contaminated zones, it continues to mutate, to haunt, and even to flourish in other ways. i visited once in 2022 and again in 2023. what you'll find there is a place defined by the sparseness of its population, human and nonhuman: toxic dust, scarce water, air that makes it hard to breathe. so who would want to live at the salton sea?
this is where I think it's so intriguing to study these marginal places. in peripheries there exists a certain kind of freedom, and a closeness to the troubles of our world, that impossible present we all live in, whether we realize it or not. in another book, Anna Tsing writes that "conjuring a future full of pasts, a ghost-ridden freedom is both a way to move on, and a way to remember." 

a future full of pasts, a ghost-ridden freedom. in bombay beach you'll walk past signs for a shoreline resort far from the current reach of the water, but there’s still signs of inhabitation amongst the dilapidated houses. people live here. some are majority-hispanic workers on nearby farms, which, by the way, still guzzle colorado river water at unsustainable rates. others are majority-white, majority-older transplants in trailers and squats. i'm not seeking to romanticize this kind of living. in imperial county, about one in five people live below the poverty line, and per capita income is roughly half the state's; the living is precarious. but it's precisely this ghost-ridden future that many come to the salton sea to experience. at least, that's what i was seeking.
and did I find it? well, i think yes. in and around the salton sea today, we find all sorts of worldmaking projects, each projecting a different future, each with its own answer to the question of what to do about a giant toxic lake and its dust.

there's restoration. the state, facing a threat not only to the valley but to all of southern california, is carving the exposed shoreline into shallow habitat ponds and planting the dry lakebed to hold the dust down. remember when i said the salton sea is kind of several lakes? this is what I meant: a lake being subdivided into engineered puddles, each one an attempt to hold back the ghost.

then there's lithium. the brine beneath the sea holds some of the richest geothermal lithium deposits on earth. gavin newsom called imperial county the saudi arabia of lithium, and the flagship project, called (i'm not making this up) hell's kitchen, promised 58,000 new jobs in a county of about 180,000 people. it has also promised the world's greenest lithium: geothermal power and extraction in a closed loop, cleaner than the hard-rock mines of australia and china or the evaporation ponds of south america. as of this month, lithium valley has yet to produce any lithium. in august, a court sent hell's kitchen's environmental review back, asking the question everyone asks here: where will the water come from, and what happens to the air as the sea dries? The workers who trained for those jobs are still waiting, and the company has started pitching its geothermal power to data centers instead (hmm). as usual, the issue is water.

water doesn't seem to be much of a concern for another world manufactured nearby: coachella. centuries ago, the fields where influencers now pose in fast-fashion brand deals were underwater, part of lake cahuilla. so here we find more contradictions. i'd hazard a guess that most coachella attendees have never heard of the salton sea, let alone lake cahuilla.

the second time i visited was in april 2023, and this time i didn't have to convince anyone. in fact, i was invited to join a large group headed to the sea itself. our destination was the bombay beach biennale, a weekend festival that one older participant told me "feels a lot like what burning nan used to be." once a year, hundreds descend on the mostly abandoned former resort town to party, showcase oddball art, and set up bizarre installations. the packing list is as you might expect: a heavy-duty mask for dust storms, lots and lots of water, and a warning that if it rains we'll be stuck for days. the experience held many of the sea's own contradictions. it was earnest and ironic, hopeful and depressed. most of the art i rather didn't like, but i had a great time dancing in the balkan techno house till sunrise.

the bombay beach biennale joins burning man and countless other parties sited on zones of discarded infrastructure. it makes sense: alternative parties have always sought out peripheral places, where rules are seldom enforced and brutality serves an aesthetic function. dancing in a dust storm has a certain rugged appeal, a chance to marvel at our own survival amidst collapse. we need these moments; a lot of my own work these days deals with the importance of partying while the world ends.

at the same time, such projects risk trivializing the material reality of the crisis. children near the sea still struggle to breathe, and toxic dust still threatens millions in southern california. they also risk repeating an old story, of the desert as a blank canvas. a Kwaaymii elder fighting to protect sacred mud pots near the sea recently put it plainly: washington sees the desert as a wasteland, but there's beauty there, and room for imagination. by telling the contradictory ghost stories of infrastructure, with site specificity like here at the salton sea, we get closer to answering what livable futures are possible amidst the ruins of our forefathers' hubris. salvaging the future will happen in places like the salton sea, where the art of living on a damaged planet is being acted out right now, and where solutions are always partial, insufficient, and contradictory.

here we are, thousands of miles from the salton sea, unthreatened by its ongoing collapse. but infrastructure is everywhere, and never infallible. the water in your glass tonight traveled over a hundred miles from reservoirs in the catskills, where whole villages were razed and flooded to make room, their foundations still down there beneath the surface. what other stories haunt the peripheries of nyc, the sites where projects of water diversion or power generation create both worlds and threats? i find it an admirable task to seek out these stories and to tell them in all our various ways: to turn our creative eyes toward the politics of despair that shadows these sites, and shine upon them, instead, something different. a collective, strange, impure, delightful, ghostly, mournful light. a promise that while we can't fix what has been wrought, we can at least choose to face it, rather than turn away and gasp in surprise as the walls around us turn to ruin.
